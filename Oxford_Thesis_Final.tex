\chapter{Introduction}
\label{chap:introduction}
One goal of generative modelling is to learn how to produce novel samples that resemble those drawn from an unknown data distribution $p_{\mathrm{data}}$. Diffusion and flow models \parencite{DDPM,Song_diffusion,diffusion_design,lipman_FM,rectified_flow} have been highly successful at this task, achieving state-of-the-art results in image, video and audio generation \parencite{rombach2022highresolutionimagesynthesislatent,ho2022videodiffusionmodels,liu2023audioldmtexttoaudiogenerationlatent}. Both use the same high-level framework for generation: samples $\mathbf{z}\sim p_{\mathrm{base}}$ drawn from a simple noise distribution are transformed continuously over time towards the data distribution \parencite{albergo2025stochasticinterpolantsunifyingframework}.

Flow and diffusion models specify the dynamics of this continuous transformation between noise and data as governed by an ordinary differential equation (ODE) and a stochastic differential equation (SDE), respectively \parencite{lipman_FM,mit_FM}. 
An ODE defines a path from a noise to data sample by specifying the local velocity at each point along it. 
In a flow model, a neural network is trained to predict this velocity. 
Generating data therefore consists of iteratively advancing a sample from noise to data through a sequence of small steps, querying the neural network at each step to determine the direction of travel.
Consequently, although flow models enable high-fidelity generation, they can often require hundreds of neural network evaluations \parencite{diffusion_design}, which can be computationally prohibitive. 

Flow maps \parencite{boffi2025flowmapmatchingstochastic,boffi2025buildconsistencymodellearning} address this limitation by training a neural network to predict the cumulative displacement between any two times along the noise-to-data paths defined by the ODE, rather than the local velocity at a single point. 
A flow map can therefore transport a noise sample directly to a data sample in a single neural network evaluation. 
Flow maps can be learned by bootsrapping from a pre-trained flow model via distillation \parencite{song2023consistencymodels,boffi2025flowmapmatchingstochastic}. However, it is also possible train a flow map from scratch, as demonstrated by MeanFlow \parencite{geng2025meanflowsonestepgenerative}. 

\section{Problem Setting and Research Gap}
\label{sec:problem_setting_and_research_gap}
Many applications require conditional generation: producing samples from the data distribution that satisfy an external condition, such as a class label or text prompt \parencite{dhariwal2021diffusionmodelsbeatgans,ho2022classifierfreediffusionguidance,rombach2022highresolutionimagesynthesislatent}. A canonical example of conditional generation is inverse problem solving \parencite{ongie2020deeplearningtechniquesinverse}, in which an observation \(\mathbf{y}\) is generated by corrupting an unknown data sample \(\mathbf{x}\sim p_{\mathrm{data}}\) according to $\mathbf{y}=\mathcal{A}(\mathbf{x})+\boldsymbol{\eta}$, where \(\mathcal{A}\) is a known forward operator and \(\boldsymbol{\eta}\) represents measurement noise. In image inverse problems, for example, \(\mathcal{A}\) may downsample, blur or mask an image. Since these operations typically discard information, multiple data samples may be consistent with the same observation. The goal of inverse problem solving is therefore to draw samples from the posterior \(p_{\mathrm{data}}(\mathbf{x}\mid\mathbf{y})\), which represents the distribution of plausible data samples consistent with the given observation \(\mathbf{y}\).

One approach to conditional generation is to train a generative model specifically for the conditional task, either from scratch or by fine-tuning a pretrained unconditional model \parencite{ho2022classifierfreediffusionguidance,meng2023distillationguideddiffusionmodels}. This can provide fast conditional generation, including one-step generation with a flow map \parencite{geng2025meanflowsonestepgenerative,chen2025visualgenerationguidance}. However, adapting the generative model to each new conditional generation setting typically requires a new round of training or fine-tuning, which can be prohibitively expensive for large generative models.

Guidance-based methods offer an alternative for flow (and diffusion) models. These methods keep the pretrained unconditional flow model frozen but nudge its velocity prediction at each sampling step, thereby steering the path from noise towards a data sample that satisfies the given condition \parencite{chung2024diffusionposteriorsamplinggeneral,song2023pseudoinverseguided,Zhang_2025,kim2025flowdpsflowdrivenposteriorsampling}. This approach hence allows for same unconditional model to be reused across different conditional generation settings without fine-tuning.

However, this guidance strategy does not transfer naturally to one-step generation with a flow map. A flow map transports an initial noise sample directly to a data sample in a single evaluation, leaving no intermediate predictions through which guidance can be applied. The key insight of Variational Flow Maps (VFM) \parencite{VFM} is that generation can instead be controlled through the choice of starting noise. For a one-step unconditional flow map, the starting noise is the only remaining lever for controlling its output. Conditional generation can therefore be reframed as learning a distribution over starting noise samples that the flow map sends to data samples satisfying the given condition.

Let $p_{\theta}(\mathbf{x})$ denote the model distribution (which approximates $p_\text{data}(\mathbf{x})$) obtained by drawing $\mathbf{z}\sim p_{\mathrm{base}}$ and passing it through the flow map, where $\theta$ denotes the neural-network parameters of the flow map.
For a given condition $\mathbf{y}$, VFM formulates the required noise distribution as the noise-space posterior $p_{\theta}(\mathbf{z}\mid\mathbf{y})$.
Intuitively, this distribution is obtained by pulling the corresponding model posterior $p_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}\mid\mathbf{y})$ backwards through the flow map into noise space.


Although \(p_{\theta}(\mathbf{z}\mid\mathbf{y})\) cannot be sampled from directly, its density can be evaluated up to a normalising constant, making variational inference (VI) \parencite{Blei_2017} a natural approach for approximating it. VI introduces a tractable distribution \(q_{\phi}(\mathbf{z}\mid\mathbf{y})\), called a variational distribution, with parameters \(\phi\), to approximate this posterior.
Fitting \(q_{\phi}(\mathbf{z}\mid\mathbf{y})\) using the variational objective requires both being able to sample from it and evaluate its density. VFM satisfies these requirements by parameterising \(q_{\phi}(\mathbf{z}\mid\mathbf{y})\) as a diagonal Gaussian whose mean and variance are predicted from \(\mathbf{y}\) by a neural network, termed the noise adapter. 

Although a Gaussian supports both sampling and exact density evaluation, it may be too simple to approximate the potentially complex noise-space posterior \(p_{\theta}(\mathbf{z}\mid\mathbf{y})\). VFM compensates for this by jointly training the noise adapter and the flow map, allowing the geometry of the noise space to be reshaped such that \(p_{\theta}(\mathbf{z}\mid\mathbf{y})\) can be more easily approximated by a simple Gaussian distribution.  
VFM therefore enables one-step conditional generation, but only by fine-tuning the flow map. This leaves open whether one-step conditional generation can be achieved while keeping the unconditional flow map fixed. 

\section{Proposed Approach and Main Findings}
\label{sec:proposed_approach_and_main_findings}
The central question of this project is whether a more expressive variational distribution $q_{\phi}(\mathbf{z}\mid\mathbf{y})$ can approximate \(p_{\theta}(\mathbf{z}\mid\mathbf{y})\) while leaving the pretrained flow map frozen. We investigate this question by replacing VFM's Gaussian noise adapter with a conditional normalising flow \parencite{VI_NF,NF_survey}. Normalising flows support both sampling and exact density evaluation, satisfying the requirements of variational training while being capable of representing substantially more complex distributions than a Gaussian. When amortising over observations $\mathbf{y}$, the inverse-problem setting also naturally permits the approximating distribution, $q_{\phi}(\mathbf{z}\mid\mathbf{y})$, to be learned via maximum likelihood estimation. We therefore introduce a training objective that combines the variational loss with a maximum-likelihood loss \parencite{BG}, and show that the combination is more effective than either loss alone.

The resulting approach enables one-step conditional generation using an existing pretrained unconditional flow map while keeping its parameters fixed. Solving each new conditional generation setting using this approach requires training only a lightweight noise adapter. We evaluate the method across inverse problems of increasing difficulty, progressing from a two-dimensional checkerboard problem to image brightness, \(4\times\) super-resolution and inpainting. On the two-dimensional problem, the Gaussian adapter fails to approximate the noise-space posterior accurately, whereas the normalising-flow adapter captures its multimodal structure. For \(4\times\) super-resolution, our method achieves the lowest maximum mean discrepancy (MMD) among all evaluated baselines, including VFM. 

However, we find that the approach is not competitive on image inpainting. Our analysis suggests that the main difficulty is learning, in a single step, which initial noise samples will map to images matching an observation that constrains many pixel values. This motivates training the adapter to predict a point partway along the noise-to-data path, rather than the initial noise itself, before mapping that point to data in a single flow-map evaluation. We leave the development of this extension to future work.

\section{Contributions}
\label{sec:contributions}

This thesis makes the following contributions:

\begin{enumerate}

\item We adapt the Variational Flow Maps (VFM) framework for one-step conditional generation so that it is training-free with respect to the flow map. In particular we replace the Gaussian noise adapter with a more expressive normalising flow. To our knowledge, this is the first work to investigate solving conditional generation entirely within the noise space of a pretrained unconditional flow map without fine-tuning the flow map itself.

\item We further extend VFM by introducing a training objective that combines both the variational loss with a maximum-likelihood loss. We show that this combined objective is more effective than training the noise adapter with either maximum likelihood or the variational loss alone.

\item We introduce a principled evaluation framework that isolates error attributable to the trained noise adapter from error attributable to the frozen pretrained flow map.

\item We demonstrate that the more expressive normalising flow adapter can substitute for flow map fine-tuning on two-dimensional checkerboard, image-brightness and \(4\times\) super-resolution inverse problems. On super-resolution, our method achieves the lowest MMD among all evaluated baselines, including VFM. However, we find that it is not competitive for image inpainting, and we provide a detailed analysis of its failure. 

\end{enumerate}

We then introduce inverse problems as the conditional generation setting considered in this thesis and relate them to the broader framework of reward alignment. \section{Generative Modelling}
\label{sec:generative_modelling}
Suppose we wish to generate examples of a particular class of object. This class may be defined as broadly or narrowly as we like. We restrict our attention to objects that can be represented as vectors $\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{D}$. In generative modelling, we assume that there exists a target probability distribution $p_{\mathrm{target}}(\mathbf{x})$ which places high density on vectors corresponding to plausible examples of the specified class of object. The goal of generative modelling is to learn an algorithm that can produce samples from this target distribution.

Often, the target distribution is a data distribution $p_{\mathrm{data}}(\mathbf{x})$ over, for example, images, videos or molecular structures. In this setting, we typically have access to a finite dataset $\mathcal{D}=\{\mathbf{x}^{(i)}\}_{i=1}^{N}$ of samples from the data distribution. Using this dataset, the aim is to learn a model distribution $p_{\theta}(\mathbf{x})$ that approximates $p_{\mathrm{data}}(\mathbf{x})$. Depending on the generative modelling algorithm, it may support sampling from $p_{\theta}(\mathbf{x})$, evaluating its density, or both. 

Many generative models, such as generative adversarial networks (GANs) \parencite{GAN} and the flow-based generative models \parencite{NF_survey,lipman_FM,geng2025meanflowsonestepgenerative} considered in this thesis, generate data by first drawing noise $\mathbf{z}\sim p_{\mathrm{base}}$ from a simple base distribution and then transforming it into a data sample $\mathbf{x}$. The learned transformation should induce a model distribution $p_{\theta}(\mathbf{x})$ that approximates the target distribution. 

\subsection{Conditional Generative Modelling}
\label{subsec:conditional_generative_modelling}

Often, we do not wish to generate an arbitrary sample from the target distribution. Instead, we wish to generate samples that satisfy a given condition $\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{M}$, where $\mathbf{y}\sim p_{Y}(\mathbf{y})$. For example, $p_{Y}$ may be a distribution over class labels, or text prompts. This task is known as conditional generative modelling. For a given condition $\mathbf{y}$, the task can be formalised as sampling from the conditional target distribution $p_{\mathrm{target}}(\mathbf{x}\mid\mathbf{y})$ \parencite{mit_FM}.

Typically, we do not want an algorithm that can generate samples for only one fixed condition. Instead, the aim is to learn an algorithm that can generate samples for different conditions $\mathbf{y}\sim p_Y(\mathbf{y})$. For example, an image generator may be required to accept different text prompts.

\subsection{Inverse Problems}
\label{subsec:inverse_problems}

Inverse problem solving is a canonical example of conditional generation and is the focus of this thesis. To define an inverse problem, we first define the corresponding forward problem. Let $\mathbf{x}\sim p_{\mathrm{data}}(\mathbf{x})$ be a data sample and let $\mathcal{A}:\mathbb{R}^{D}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{M}$ be a known forward operator. The forward operator acts on $\mathbf{x}$ to produce an observation
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{y}
=
\mathcal{A}(\mathbf{x})+\boldsymbol{\eta},
\qquad
\boldsymbol{\eta}
\sim
\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\sigma_y^2\mathbf{I}_{M})
\label{eq:forward_problem}
\end{equation}
where $\boldsymbol{\eta}$ represents measurement noise. In image inverse problems, for example, $\mathcal{A}$ may blur, downsample or mask an image. The forward problem consists of computing the observation $\mathbf{y}$ from the data sample $\mathbf{x}$ \parencite{ongie2020deeplearningtechniquesinverse}.

The inverse problem is the reverse problem. Given an observation $\mathbf{y}$, the aim is to infer the unknown data sample $\mathbf{x}$ from which it was produced. However, the forward operator typically destroys information and is therefore not invertible. Several data samples may consequently be consistent with the same observation. Therefore, bayesian inverse problem solving instead aims to draw samples from the posterior distribution
\begin{equation}
p_{\mathrm{data}}(\mathbf{x}\mid\mathbf{y})
=
\frac{
p(\mathbf{y}\mid\mathbf{x})
p_{\mathrm{data}}(\mathbf{x})
}{
p_Y(\mathbf{y})
}
\label{eq:inverse_problem_posterior}
\end{equation}
where $p(\mathbf{y}\mid\mathbf{x})$ is the likelihood of observing $\mathbf{y}$ given $\mathbf{x}$. Under the Gaussian measurement model in Equation~\ref{eq:forward_problem}, the likelihood is
\begin{equation}
p(\mathbf{y}\mid\mathbf{x})
=
\mathcal{N}\left(
\mathbf{y}
\mid
\mathcal{A}(\mathbf{x}),
\sigma_y^2\mathbf{I}_{M}
\right)
\propto
\exp\left(
-\frac{
\lVert\mathbf{y}-\mathcal{A}(\mathbf{x})\rVert_2^2
}{
2\sigma_y^2
}
\right)
\label{eq:inverse_problem_likelihood}
\end{equation}
Substituting the likelihood into Equation~\ref{eq:inverse_problem_posterior} gives
\begin{equation}
p_{\mathrm{data}}(\mathbf{x}\mid\mathbf{y})
\propto
p_{\mathrm{data}}(\mathbf{x})
\exp\left(
-\frac{
\lVert\mathbf{y}-\mathcal{A}(\mathbf{x})\rVert_2^2
}{
2\sigma_y^2
}
\right)
\label{eq:inverse_problem_posterior_gaussian}
\end{equation}
The posterior therefore assigns high density to samples that are both plausible under $p_{\mathrm{data}}(\mathbf{x})$ and consistent with the observation $\mathbf{y}$.

\subsection{Relation to Reward Alignment}
\label{subsec:relation_to_reward_alignment}
Conditional generation can be viewed as a particular instance of the broader task of reward alignment. This connection allows us to discuss general approaches for reward alignment in the related-work chapter with the view that they can be applied to conditional generation tasks, such as inverse problem solving.

Reward alignment aims to generate samples from the target distribution with specified desirable properties, as quantified by a reward function $r(\mathbf{x})$. For example, a reward function may compute an aesthetic rating of an image. Reward alignment can be formalised as sampling from 
\begin{equation}
p_{\mathrm{reward}}(\mathbf{x})
\propto
p_{\mathrm{target}}(\mathbf{x})
\exp\left(r(\mathbf{x})\right)
\label{eq:reward_tilted_distribution}
\end{equation}
This distribution reweights the target distribution towards samples with higher reward, and is known as the reward-tilted distribution \parencite{huang2026guideflowfewstepalignment,MFM,Diamond_maps}.

To express conditional generation as reward alignment, we let each condition $\mathbf{y}$ define a corresponding reward function $r_{\mathbf{y}}(\mathbf{x})$. In particular, we choose this reward to be the log-likelihood of the condition given the sample $r_{\mathbf{y}}(\mathbf{x}) = \log p(\mathbf{y}\mid\mathbf{x})$
The corresponding reward-tilted distribution is
\begin{equation}
p_{\mathrm{reward}}(\mathbf{x}\mid\mathbf{y})
\propto
p_{\mathrm{target}}(\mathbf{x})
p(\mathbf{y}\mid\mathbf{x})
\propto
p_{\mathrm{target}}(\mathbf{x}\mid\mathbf{y})
\label{eq:conditional_generation_as_reward_alignment}
\end{equation}
For the inverse problems defined above, $p(\mathbf{y}\mid\mathbf{x})$ is the likelihood in Equation~\ref{eq:inverse_problem_likelihood}. In this example, samples receive a high reward when they are consistent with the observation. Inverse problem solving is therefore a particular instance of conditional generation, which can itself be formulated as reward alignment \parencite{VFM}.

\section{Flow-Based Generative Modelling}
\label{sec:flow_based_generative_modelling}
Flow-based generative models transform samples from a tractable base distribution $p_{\mathrm{base}}(\mathbf{z})$, typically a standard normal $\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{I})$, into samples from a target distribution $p_{\mathrm{target}}(\mathbf{x})$.
Normalising flows represent this transformation as a finite composition of invertible maps \parencite{NF_survey}. Flow models and flow maps represent it as a continuous transformation governed by an ODE. While flow models learn the vector field defining the ODE \parencite{lipman_FM,mit_FM}, flow maps instead aim to directly learn the integral of the ODE \parencite{sander_MF}.

These models play different roles in this thesis. A pretrained flow map acts as the generative model that maps noise to data. Whereas we use a normalising flow to learn a conditional distribution over the noise space of the flow model (i.e. the noise adapter). Flow models provide the continuous-time transport framework from which flow maps are defined.

\subsection{Normalising Flows}
\label{subsec:normalising_flows}
% We now give an overview of normalising flows because noise adapter in our method is chosen to be a normalising flow. We make this choice because normalising flows can represent complex distributions while supporting both sampling and exact density evaluation, enabling variational training of the noise adapter. 
We first define normalising flows and explain how their densities are evaluated. We then introduce autoregressive flows, which are the specific variation of normalising flows that we use to implement the noise adapter. Finally we explain how normalising flows can be learned using forward or reverse KL divergence objectives. Our presentation and terminology follow the review of \textcite{NF_survey}, to which we refer for proofs and a broader survey of architectures.

\subsubsection{Definition and Change of Variables}
\label{subsubsec:definition_and_change_of_variables}
Suppose we wish to model a target distribution $p_{\mathrm{target}}(\mathbf{x})$. A normalising flow begins with a simple base distribution $p_{\mathrm{base}}(\mathbf{z})$ from which we can easily sample and whose density can be evaluated exactly. It then learns a transformation $T_{\phi}:\mathbb{R}^{D}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{D}$ that maps samples from this base distribution to samples whose distribution approximates the target distribution. More precisely, samples are generated according to
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{z}
\sim
p_{\mathrm{base}}(\mathbf{z})
\qquad
\mathbf{x}
=
T_{\phi}(\mathbf{z})
\qquad
\mathbf{x}
\sim
p_{\phi}(\mathbf{x})
\label{eq:normalising_flow_transformation}
\end{equation}
and the parameters $\phi$ of the transformation are learned such that $p_{\phi}(\mathbf{x})$ approximates $p_{\mathrm{target}}(\mathbf{x})$ \parencite{NF_survey}.

A normalising flow requires $T_{\phi}$ to be a diffeomorphism: an invertible, continuously differentiable map whose inverse is also continuously differentiable. Invertibility means that every point $\mathbf{x}$ corresponds to a unique base point $\mathbf{z}=T_{\phi}^{-1}(\mathbf{x})$.

Because $T_{\phi}$ is a diffeomorphism, the density of $p_{\phi}$ is well-defined and can be evaluated using the change-of-variables formula
\begin{equation}
p_{\phi}(\mathbf{x})
=
p_{\mathrm{base}}\left(
T_{\phi}^{-1}(\mathbf{x})
\right)
\left|
\det
\frac{
\partial T_{\phi}^{-1}(\mathbf{x})
}{
\partial \mathbf{x}
}
\right|
\label{eq:normalising_flow_change_of_variables}
\end{equation}
To evaluate the density according to Equation~\ref{eq:normalising_flow_change_of_variables}, the inverse transformation must not only exist but also be computationally tractable. Intuitively, Equation~\ref{eq:normalising_flow_change_of_variables} states that in order to compute the density of $\mathbf{x} \sim p_{\phi}$, first consider mapping $\mathbf{x}$ back to its corresponding base point $\mathbf{z}=T_{\phi}^{-1}(\mathbf{x})$. Subsequently, the density at $\mathbf{x}$ is equal to the base density at $\mathbf{z}$, weighted according to the local change in volume produced by $T_{\phi}$. If $T_{\phi}$ expands the space around $\mathbf{z}$, the same probability mass is spread across a larger region and the density decreases. If it contracts the space, the density increases.

When $\mathbf{x}$ has been generated from a known base point $\mathbf{z}$, the same density can instead be evaluated using the Jacobian of the forward transformation
\begin{equation}
p_{\phi}(\mathbf{x})
=
p_{\mathrm{base}}(\mathbf{z})
\left|
\det
\frac{
\partial T_{\phi}(\mathbf{z})
}{
\partial \mathbf{z}
}
\right|^{-1}
\qquad
\mathbf{x}
=
T_{\phi}(\mathbf{z})
\label{eq:normalising_flow_forward_change_of_variables}
\end{equation}
This form avoids evaluating $T_{\phi}^{-1}$ because the corresponding base point $\mathbf{z}$ is already known. Both forms are needed in this thesis: the inverse form in Equation~\ref{eq:normalising_flow_change_of_variables} underlies the forward-KL objective, and this forward form underlies the reverse-KL objective, both of which we use to train the noise adapter and introduce later. 

A normalising flow therefore supports both sampling and exact density evaluation. However, for an unrestricted $D\times D$ Jacobian, computing the determinant in Equation~\ref{eq:normalising_flow_change_of_variables} requires $\mathcal{O}(D^3)$ operations.

\subsubsection{Constructing Expressive and Tractable Flows}
\label{subsubsec:constructing_expressive_and_tractable_flows}
In practice, $T_{\phi}$ is parameterised by a neural network. Since an arbitrary neural network is neither guaranteed to be invertible nor to have a tractable Jacobian determinant, its functional form must be restricted to guarantee these properties, without limiting its ability to represent complex target distributions.

A common approach is to construct $T_{\phi}$ as a composition of $L$ relatively simple invertible transformations, which we refer to as flow layers
\begin{equation}
T_{\phi}
=
T_{\phi_L}
\circ
T_{\phi_{L-1}}
\circ
\cdots
\circ
T_{\phi_1}
\label{eq:NF_composition}
\end{equation}
where $\phi=(\phi_1,\ldots,\phi_L)$. Starting from $\mathbf{z}_0=\mathbf{z}$, each layer is applied in turn as $\mathbf{z}_{\ell}=T_{\phi_\ell}(\mathbf{z}_{\ell-1})$, with $\mathbf{z}_L=\mathbf{x}$. Importantly, a composition of diffeomorphisms is itself a diffeomorphism, whose inverse is obtained by applying the inverse layers in reverse order.

By the chain rule, the Jacobian determinant of this composition is the product of the Jacobian determinants of its layers, so its log-determinant is the sum of the layers' log-determinants. Hence, if each layer has an efficient inverse and Jacobian determinant, so does the overall flow. Stacking more layers increases the expressiveness of $T_{\phi}$, while the cost of evaluating its log-determinant grows only linearly with $L$ \parencite{NF_survey}.

Several approaches have been developed for constructing flow layers that are invertible, expressive and with cheap Jacobian determinant. These include autoregressive flows, linear flows and residual flows \parencite{NF_survey}. We focus on autoregressive flows because they have been shown to scale effectively to generative modelling for high-dimensional data, including images \parencite{TarFlow}.

\subsubsection{Autoregressive Flow Layers}
\label{subsubsec:autoregressive_flow_layers}
Consider a single flow layer $T_{\phi_\ell}$. To simplify notation,
denote its input by $\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{D}$ and its output by
$\mathbf{z}'=T_{\phi_\ell}(\mathbf{z})\in\mathbb{R}^{D}$.
An autoregressive flow layer splits the coordinates of $\mathbf{z}$
into $K$ equal-sized blocks
$\mathbf{z}_1,\ldots,\mathbf{z}_K$. The output $\mathbf{z}'$ is split
into corresponding blocks
$\mathbf{z}'_1,\ldots,\mathbf{z}'_K$. Each output block
$\mathbf{z}'_i$ is obtained by applying an elementwise transformation
to the corresponding input block $\mathbf{z}_i$. The parameters of
this transformation may depend only on the preceding input blocks
$\mathbf{z}_{<i}=(\mathbf{z}_1,\ldots,\mathbf{z}_{i-1})$. In
particular,
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{z}'_i
=
\tau\left(\mathbf{z}_i;\mathbf{h}_i\right)
\qquad
\mathbf{h}_i
=
c_i\left(\mathbf{z}_{<i}\right)
\qquad
i=1,\ldots,K
\label{eq:autoregressive_flow_layer}
\end{equation}
The function $\tau$, called the transformer, performs the elementwise
transformation of $\mathbf{z}_i$. Its parameters $\mathbf{h}_i$ are
predicted by the $i$th conditioner $c_i$, which is typically
implemented using a neural network. Here, transformer refers to the
function that transforms each block, rather than the Transformer
neural-network architecture.

For the layer to be invertible, the transformer $\tau$ must be invertible with respect to $\mathbf{z}_i$ when its parameters $\mathbf{h}_i$ are fixed. This is achieved by making $\tau$ strictly monotonic in $\mathbf{z}_i$. The conditioners, by contrast, need not be invertible.

Under these conditions, the layer can be inverted block by block: $\mathbf{h}_1$ depends on no input blocks, so $\mathbf{z}_1=\tau^{-1}(\mathbf{z}'_1;\mathbf{h}_1)$, and each subsequent $\mathbf{z}_i$ can be recovered once $\mathbf{z}_{<i}$ is known \parencite{NF_survey}. Inverting $T_{\phi_\ell}$ therefore requires $K$ sequential steps, each evaluating the conditioner and the transformer.

Because $\tau$ acts elementwise and $\mathbf{h}_i$ depends only on $\mathbf{z}_{<i}$, each output block depends only on its own input block and the preceding ones, so the Jacobian of the layer is lower-triangular. Its log-determinant is therefore a sum of $D$ diagonal terms, computable in $\mathcal{O}(D)$ time \parencite{NF_survey}. This matters for the noise adapter, whose noise space has dimension $D=3072$ for CIFAR-10, where an $\mathcal{O}(D^3)$ determinant at every training step would be prohibitive.

\subsubsection{Computational Direction: IAF and MAF}
\label{subsubsec:computational_direction_iaf_and_maf}
Sampling from a normalising flow applies each layer $T_{\phi_\ell}$ in turn, whereas evaluating a density via Equation~\ref{eq:normalising_flow_change_of_variables} applies the inverse layers. We showed above that inverting an autoregressive layer requires $K$ sequential steps. By contrast, the forward transformation $\mathbf{z}'=T_{\phi_\ell}(\mathbf{z})$ can be evaluated in parallel. Given $\mathbf{z}$, all input blocks are already known, so every $\mathbf{h}_i=c_i(\mathbf{z}_{<i})$ can be computed simultaneously. In practice, the conditioners share one neural network that maps $\mathbf{z}$ to all of $\mathbf{h}_1,\ldots,\mathbf{h}_K$ in a single forward pass, with its weights masked to remove any path from $\mathbf{z}_{\geq i}$ to $\mathbf{h}_i$ \parencite{germain2015mademaskedautoencoderdistribution,NF_survey}. Every output block $\mathbf{z}'_i=\tau(\mathbf{z}_i;\mathbf{h}_i)$ can then also be computed simultaneously. Sampling is therefore comparatively inexpensive, whereas density evaluation is more expensive.

If an application instead prioritises fast density evaluation, we can instead allow the parameters to be computed from the output blocks, $\mathbf{h}_i=c_i(\mathbf{z}'_{<i})$. With this change, the layer remains invertible with a lower-triangular Jacobian, but the costs are reversed: $T_{\phi_\ell}^{-1}$ can now be evaluated in parallel, whereas $T_{\phi_\ell}$ requires $K$ sequential steps.

Computing the parameters from the input blocks $\mathbf{z}_{<i}$ gives an inverse autoregressive flow (IAF) \parencite{kingma2017improvingvariationalinferenceinverse}, which provides fast sampling. Computing them from the output blocks $\mathbf{z}'_{<i}$ gives a masked autoregressive flow (MAF) \parencite{papamakarios2018maskedautoregressiveflowdensity}, which provides fast density evaluation. This distinction matters for the noise adapter, since reverse-KL training relies on sampling, whereas forward-KL training relies on density evaluation.

\subsubsection{Transformer Autoregressive Flows}
\label{subsubsec:transformer_autoregressive_flows}
We now introduce the normalising-flow architecture used to represent
the noise adapter in this project: Transformer Autoregressive Flow
(TarFlow) \parencite{TarFlow}. TarFlow constructs the overall
normalising flow as a composition of $L$ masked autoregressive flow
layers. It adapts the original MAF architecture
\parencite{papamakarios2018maskedautoregressiveflowdensity} to better model distributions over images. It achieves this through four main design
choices. We explain each of these choices in turn.

The first design choice determines how the image-shaped variable $\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{C\times H\times W}$, with $C$ channels, height $H$ and width $W$, is divided into blocks. TarFlow splits the image into non-overlapping $P\times P$ patches spanning all channels, and flattens each patch into a block of $CP^2$ coordinates, which it calls a token. This gives $K=(H/P)(W/P)$ tokens, initially ordered by scanning the image from left to right and top to bottom.

The second design choice specifies the transformer $\tau$ applied to
each token. TarFlow uses the elementwise affine transformation
\begin{equation}
\tau\left(\mathbf{z}_i;\mathbf{h}_i\right)
=
\exp\left(\boldsymbol{\alpha}_i\right)
\odot
\mathbf{z}_i
+
\boldsymbol{\beta}_i
\qquad
\mathbf{h}_i
=
\left(
\boldsymbol{\alpha}_i,
\boldsymbol{\beta}_i
\right)
\label{eq:tarflow_affine_transformer}
\end{equation}
where
$\boldsymbol{\alpha}_i,\boldsymbol{\beta}_i
\in\mathbb{R}^{CP^2}$ and $\odot$ denotes elementwise multiplication.
The exponential ensures that every scale is positive, so the
transformation is invertible.

% The third design choice specifies the conditioner $c_i$. Recall that,
% in a masked autoregressive flow, the transformation parameters are
% computed via $\mathbf{h}_i=c_i(\mathbf{z}'_{<i})$. TarFlow uses a
% single causal Transformer neural network to compute
% $\mathbf{h}_1,\ldots,\mathbf{h}_K$. Each token $\mathbf{z}'_i$ is
% first projected from dimension $CP^2$ to the hidden dimension of the
% Transformer, and a learned positional embedding is added. The
% resulting sequence is then processed by a stack of Transformer layers.
% Each layer contains a causal multi-head self-attention layer followed
% by a multilayer perceptron layer. The causal attention mask prevents
% each token from attending to later tokens. A final linear projection
% maps each token embedding to the affine parameters
% $(\boldsymbol{\alpha}_i,\boldsymbol{\beta}_i)$. 

The third design choice specifies the conditioner. Recall that, in a masked autoregressive flow, the parameters of token $i$ are computed as $\mathbf{h}_i=c_i(\mathbf{z}'_{<i})$. Rather than using a separate network for each $c_i$, TarFlow computes all of $\mathbf{h}_1,\ldots,\mathbf{h}_K$ with a single causal Transformer network, with one such network per flow layer. This computation proceeds in three steps. First, each token $\mathbf{z}'_i\in\mathbb{R}^{CP^2}$ is projected to the hidden dimension $d$ of the Transformer by a learned matrix $W_{\mathrm{in}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times CP^2}$, and a learned positional embedding $\mathbf{p}_i\in\mathbb{R}^{d}$ is added, giving the embedding $\mathbf{e}_i$. Second, the sequence $\mathbf{e}_1,\ldots,\mathbf{e}_K$ is processed by $\mathsf{Transformer}$, which denotes a stack of Transformer blocks, each consisting of causal multi-head self-attention followed by a multilayer perceptron. This produces an output $\mathbf{u}_i\in\mathbb{R}^{d}$ at every position. Third, a learned matrix $W_{\mathrm{out}}$ maps the output at position $i-1$ to the affine parameters of token $i$. In summary,
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\mathbf{e}_i
&=
W_{\mathrm{in}}\,\mathbf{z}'_i+\mathbf{p}_i
\\
(\mathbf{u}_1,\ldots,\mathbf{u}_K)
&=
\mathsf{Transformer}(\mathbf{e}_1,\ldots,\mathbf{e}_K)
\\
\mathbf{h}_i=(\boldsymbol{\alpha}_i,\boldsymbol{\beta}_i)
&=
W_{\mathrm{out}}\,\mathbf{u}_{i-1}
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:tarflow_conditioner}
\end{equation}
Because of the causal attention mask, the output $\mathbf{u}_i$ depends only on the tokens $\mathbf{z}'_{\leq i}$. Computing $\mathbf{h}_i$ from the output at the preceding position, $\mathbf{u}_{i-1}$, therefore ensures that $\mathbf{h}_i$ depends only on $\mathbf{z}'_{<i}$, as a masked autoregressive flow requires. The first token has no preceding position, so its parameters are set to $\mathbf{h}_1=(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{0})$. By Equation~\ref{eq:tarflow_affine_transformer}, this gives $\mathbf{z}'_1=\exp(\mathbf{0})\odot\mathbf{z}_1+\mathbf{0}=\mathbf{z}_1$, so each flow layer passes its first token through unchanged.

The fourth design choice concerns the token ordering. TarFlow reverses the order of the tokens between successive flow layers, so that tokens which appear early in one layer appear late in the next. Since each token is conditioned only on the tokens before it, this reversal allows every token to be conditioned on every other token across layers. Together, these design choices result in a scalable normalising flow architecture for modelling image distributions. In our image inverse problem experiments, we choose TarFlow to implement our noise adapter because the noise adapter models a distribution over the image-shaped noise space of a flow map.

\subsubsection{Training Normalising Flows}
\label{subsubsec:training_normalising_flows}
So far, we have described how to construct a normalising flow $T_{\phi}$ that induces a model distribution $p_{\phi}(\mathbf{x})$. We now discuss how to learn $\phi$ such that $p_{\phi}(\mathbf{x})$ approximates $p_{\mathrm{target}}(\mathbf{x})$. A natural approach is to minimise the KL divergence between the two distributions. Since the KL divergence is asymmetric, it can be minimised in either the forward or the reverse direction.

We first define the forward KL loss as
\begin{align}
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{fKL}}(\phi)
:=
D_{\mathrm{KL}}\left(p_{\mathrm{target}}\parallel p_{\phi}\right)
&=
\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}\sim p_{\mathrm{target}}}
\left[
\log\frac{p_{\mathrm{target}}(\mathbf{x})}{p_{\phi}(\mathbf{x})}
\right]
\nonumber
\\
&=
-\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}\sim p_{\mathrm{target}}}
\left[\log p_{\phi}(\mathbf{x})\right]
+
\mathrm{const}
\label{eq:normalising_flow_forward_kl}
\end{align}

% \begin{align}
% \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{fKL}}(\phi)
% &:=
% D_{\mathrm{KL}}\left(p_{\mathrm{target}}\parallel p_{\phi}\right)
% =
% \mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}\sim p_{\mathrm{target}}}
% \left[
% \log\frac{p_{\mathrm{target}}(\mathbf{x})}{p_{\phi}(\mathbf{x})}
% \right]
% \nonumber
% \\
% &=
% -\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}\sim p_{\mathrm{target}}}
% \left[\log p_{\phi}(\mathbf{x})\right]
% +
% \mathrm{const}
% \label{eq:normalising_flow_forward_kl}
% \end{align}
The constant is the expected log target density, which does not depend on $\phi$. Minimising
$\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{fKL}}$ is therefore equivalent to maximising the
expected log-likelihood assigned by the normalising flow to samples
from the target distribution. This is precisely maximum-likelihood training.
Estimating this loss requires samples $\mathbf{x}\sim p_{\mathrm{target}}$ and the ability to evaluate $p_{\phi}(\mathbf{x})$ at those samples. By the change-of-variables formula in Equation~\ref{eq:normalising_flow_change_of_variables}, evaluating $p_{\phi}(\mathbf{x})$ requires computing $T_{\phi}^{-1}(\mathbf{x})$ and its Jacobian determinant. Importantly, it does not require evaluating the target density $p_{\mathrm{target}}$.

We next define the reverse KL loss as
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{rKL}}(\phi)
:=
D_{\mathrm{KL}}
\left(
p_{\phi}
\parallel
p_{\mathrm{target}}
\right)
=
\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}\sim p_{\phi}}
\left[
\log p_{\phi}(\mathbf{x})
-
\log p_{\mathrm{target}}(\mathbf{x})
\right]
\label{eq:normalising_flow_reverse_kl}
\end{equation}
The expectation in
Equation~\ref{eq:normalising_flow_reverse_kl} is taken over
$\mathbf{x}\sim p_{\phi}$. Since $p_{\phi}$ is obtained by
transforming the base distribution through $T_{\phi}$, the standard
reparameterisation trick \parencite{pml2Book} allows this expectation
to be rewritten over $\mathbf{z}\sim p_{\mathrm{base}}$. Substituting
the forward change-of-variables formula
\ref{eq:normalising_flow_forward_change_of_variables} then gives
\begin{align}
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{rKL}}(\phi)
&=
\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{z}\sim p_{\mathrm{base}}}
\left[
\log p_{\phi}
\left(
T_{\phi}(\mathbf{z})
\right)
-
\log p_{\mathrm{target}}
\left(
T_{\phi}(\mathbf{z})
\right)
\right]
\nonumber
\\
&=
\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{z}\sim p_{\mathrm{base}}}
\left[
\log p_{\mathrm{base}}(\mathbf{z})
-
\log
\left|
\det
\frac{
\partial T_{\phi}(\mathbf{z})
}{
\partial \mathbf{z}
}
\right|
-
\log p_{\mathrm{target}}
\left(
T_{\phi}(\mathbf{z})
\right)
\right]
\label{eq:normalising_flow_reverse_kl_reparameterised}
\end{align}
Equation~\ref{eq:normalising_flow_reverse_kl_reparameterised} shows
that reverse-KL training requires sampling from
$p_{\mathrm{base}}$, evaluating $T_{\phi}$ and its Jacobian
determinant, and evaluating the target density at the generated
sample. Unlike forward-KL training, it does not require samples from
$p_{\mathrm{target}}$. Furthermore, an unnormalised target density is
sufficient because its unknown normalising constant is independent of
$\phi$ and therefore does not affect optimisation \parencite{NF_survey}.

The two objectives also behave differently when $p_{\mathrm{target}}$ is multimodal. Forward KL takes its expectation over $p_{\mathrm{target}}$, so any region where $p_{\mathrm{target}}$ has mass but $p_{\phi}$ assigns little density incurs a very large penalty $-\log p_{\phi}(\mathbf{x})$. Forward KL is therefore mode-covering: it forces $p_{\phi}$ to cover every mode, even at the cost of placing mass between modes. Reverse KL takes its expectation over $p_{\phi}$, so it penalises placing mass where $p_{\mathrm{target}}$ has none, but not missing a mode. Reverse KL is therefore mode-seeking, and often concentrates $p_{\phi}$ on a subset of the target's modes \parencite{BG}.

\subsection{Flow Models and Flow Matching}
\label{subsec:flow_models_and_flow_matching}
Flow models describe the transformation from noise to data as a continuous-time process, rather than as a finite chain of invertible maps. Instead of learning the entire transformation at once, a flow model only needs to specify how a sample should move locally at each point in time. We index time by $t\in[0,1]$ and adopt the convention of MeanFlow \parencite{geng2025meanflowsonestepgenerative}: a sample is drawn from the base distribution at $t=1$ and is continuously transformed into a sample from the target distribution at $t=0$. The definitions and results in this subsection follow \textcite{mit_FM} and \textcite{lipman_FM}.

\subsubsection{ODEs and Trajectories}
\label{subsubsec:odes_and_trajectories}
The local motion of a point is specified by a vector field $v:\mathbb{R}^{D}\times[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}^{D}$, which assigns a velocity $v(\mathbf{x},t)$ to every location $\mathbf{x}$ and time $t$. To describe how a point starting at $\mathbf{z}$ moves under this vector field, let $\mathbf{x}_t$ denote its location at time $t$. Its motion is governed by the ordinary differential equation (ODE)
\begin{equation}
\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}_t}{\mathrm{d}t}
=
v(\mathbf{x}_t,t)
\qquad
\mathbf{x}_1=\mathbf{z}
\label{eq:ode}
\end{equation}
which states that the point starts at $\mathbf{z}$ at time $t=1$ and, as $t$ decreases, always moves with the velocity that the vector field specifies at its current location and time. The resulting path $(\mathbf{x}_t)_{t\in[0,1]}$ is called a trajectory, and is the solution of the ODE.

If $v$ is continuously differentiable with bounded derivatives, the Picard--Lindel\"of theorem guarantees that, for every starting point $\mathbf{z}$, exactly one trajectory satisfies Equation~\ref{eq:ode} \parencite{mit_FM}. Each starting point therefore determines a single unique trajectory, and hence a single end point $\mathbf{x}_0$. This uniqueness is what later makes flow maps well defined.

\subsubsection{Flow Models}
\label{subsubsec:flow_models}
The ODE is deterministic, so randomness enters only through the starting point. We denote random variables by capital letters. Suppose the starting point is drawn at random, $\mathbf{X}_1\sim p_{\mathrm{base}}$, and then follows the vector field according to Equation~\ref{eq:ode}. For each $t$, its location $\mathbf{X}_t$ along the resulting trajectory is a deterministic function of the random starting point, and is therefore itself a random variable, with some distribution $p_t$. As $t$ decreases from 1 to 0, following the vector field therefore transports $p_1=p_{\mathrm{base}}$ through a continuous sequence of distributions $p_t$, ending at $p_0$.

A flow model parameterises the vector field by a neural network $v_{\theta}$ with parameters $\theta$. Since our ultimate goal is to sample from $p_{\mathrm{target}}$, $\theta$ is learned so that the distribution of the end point $\mathbf{X}_0$, which we denote $p_{\theta}$, approximates $p_{\mathrm{target}}$. Following $v_{\theta}$ from fresh noise $\mathbf{X}_1\sim p_{\mathrm{base}}$ then produces new samples distributed according to an approximation of $p_{\mathrm{target}}$ at time $t=0$.

\subsubsection{Sampling}
\label{subsubsec:sampling}
In practice, $v_{\theta}$ cannot be followed exactly, because the ODE has no closed-form solution when the vector field is a neural network. Instead, the trajectory is followed in discrete steps. The simplest scheme is Euler's method, which starts from $\mathbf{x}_1=\mathbf{z}$ with $\mathbf{z}\sim p_{\mathrm{base}}$, divides $[0,1]$ into $n$ steps of size $h=1/n$, and at each step moves the sample a short distance along its current velocity:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{x}_{t-h}
=
\mathbf{x}_t-h\,v_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_t,t)
\qquad
t=1,1-h,\ldots,h
\label{eq:euler}
\end{equation}
The velocity is subtracted because time runs from $t=1$ to $t=0$. Smaller steps follow $v_{\theta}$ more accurately, but each step costs one network function evaluation (NFE), so accurate sampling can require many evaluations. This is the cost that flow maps are designed to remove.

\subsubsection{Flow Matching}
\label{subsubsec:flow_matching}
We want to learn a vector field $v_{\theta}$ that transports $\mathbf{X}_1\sim p_{\mathrm{base}}$ to $\mathbf{X}_0\sim p_{\theta}$, such that $p_{\theta}$ approximates $p_{\mathrm{target}}$. However, this requirement only constrains the two ends of the transformation. Many different vector fields transport $p_{\mathrm{base}}$ to $p_{\mathrm{target}}$, and they differ in the intermediate distributions $p_t$ that the samples pass through on the way. Flow matching \parencite{lipman_FM} removes this ambiguity by explicitly constructing a target sequence of distributions $p_t$, called a probability path, together with a vector field that moves samples along it. That is, if we draw samples from $p_{\mathrm{base}}$ and follow this constructed vector field, the samples are distributed according to the constructed probability path $p_t$ at every time $t$. This constructed vector field is called the marginal vector field, and flow matching trains $v_{\theta}$ to match it.

In the flow matching framework, constructing $p_t$ boils down to choosing a 'conditional probability path'. We choose the CondOT conditional probability path in this thesis, and refer to \textcite{mit_FM} for details regarding the exact construction of $p_t$. Given this choice, $p_t$ is very simple. A sample from $p_t$ is obtained by drawing a data point $\mathbf{x}_0\sim p_{\mathrm{target}}$ and a noise sample $\mathbf{x}_1\sim p_{\mathrm{base}}$ independently, and taking the point a fraction $t$ of the way along the straight line from $\mathbf{x}_0$ to $\mathbf{x}_1$:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{x}_t
=
(1-t)\,\mathbf{x}_0+t\,\mathbf{x}_1
\label{eq:condot_interpolation}
\end{equation}
At $t=0$ this point is the data sample and at $t=1$ it is the noise sample, so $p_0=p_{\mathrm{target}}$ and $p_1=p_{\mathrm{base}}$, as required. Importantly, this straight line is only a way of drawing samples from $p_t$. It does not describe the trajectories of the marginal vector field, which are generally curved rather than straight.

Having fixed $p_t$, flow matching trains $v_{\theta}$ using a simple regression objective. We sample a time $t\sim\mathcal{U}[0,1]$, a data point $\mathbf{x}_0\sim p_{\mathrm{target}}$ and a noise sample $\mathbf{x}_1\sim p_{\mathrm{base}}$, construct $\mathbf{x}_t$ using Equation~\ref{eq:condot_interpolation}, and regress the output of the network at $\mathbf{x}_t$ against the velocity of the straight line, $\mathbf{x}_1-\mathbf{x}_0$:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{FM}}(\theta)
=
\mathbb{E}_{t,\,\mathbf{x}_0,\,\mathbf{x}_1}
\left\|
v_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_t,t)
-
(\mathbf{x}_1-\mathbf{x}_0)
\right\|^2
\label{eq:flow_matching_loss}
\end{equation}
In practice, this expectation is estimated by averaging over a minibatch of sampled triples $(t,\mathbf{x}_0,\mathbf{x}_1)$. Although the training algorithm is simple, showing that its minimiser is the marginal vector field is fairly involved. We refer readers to \textcite{mit_FM} and \textcite{lipman_FM} for the proof. 

\subsection{Flow Maps}
\label{subsec:flow_maps}
Our presentation of flow maps follows \textcite{sander_MF} and \textcite{boffi2025flowmapmatchingstochastic}.

\subsubsection{Motivation and Definition}
\label{subsubsec:flow_map_definition}
Recall from Section~\ref{subsubsec:sampling} that generating a sample with a flow model requires simulating a trajectory from noise to data in many small Euler steps, each of which requires one evaluation of the velocity network $v_\theta$. Sampling from a flow model is therefore expensive.

Consider a point on a trajectory, at location $\mathbf{x}_s$ at time $s$. If we follow the vector field $v$ exactly from time $s$ to another time $t$, taking infinitesimally small steps along the trajectory, the point $\mathbf{x}_s$ is displaced by exactly the integral $\int_s^t v(\mathbf{x}_\tau,\tau)\,\mathrm{d}\tau$. By taking small steps in the direction output by the velocity network, as in Euler's method, we are effectively approximating this integral. This motivates training a neural network to learn the integral of the marginal vector field directly, rather than the vector field itself. In particular, we want to learn the flow map
\begin{equation}
F(\mathbf{x}_s,s,t)
=
\mathbf{x}_s+\int_{s}^{t}v(\mathbf{x}_\tau,\tau)\,\mathrm{d}\tau
\label{eq:flow_map}
\end{equation}
which takes as input the location $\mathbf{x}_s$ of a point at time $s$ on a trajectory and outputs the location $\mathbf{x}_t$ at time $t$ on that same trajectory. If a neural network $F_{\theta}$ has been successfully trained to predict the flow map, we can draw $\mathbf{z}\sim p_{\mathrm{base}}$ and evaluate the one-step generator $G_{\theta}(\mathbf{z}):=F_{\theta}(\mathbf{z},1,0)$ to obtain a sample at $t=0$. Sampling therefore requires only a single forward pass of the network.

\subsubsection{Well-Definedness and the Noise--Data Bijection}
\label{subsubsec:flow_map_bijection}
For the flow map to be a well-defined function, the inputs $\mathbf{x}_s$, $s$ and $t$ must determine exactly one output. Two things must therefore hold. First, the point $\mathbf{x}_s$ at time $s$ must lie on only one trajectory, since otherwise it would be unclear which trajectory to follow. Second, following that trajectory to time $t$ must lead to only one location. Both follow from the Picard--Lindel\"of theorem introduced in Section~\ref{subsubsec:odes_and_trajectories}. 

A consequence of the Picard--Lindel\"of theorem is that the vector field $v$ defines a bijection between noise samples $\mathbf{z}\sim p_{\mathrm{base}}$ and target samples $\mathbf{x}\sim p_{\mathrm{target}}$. Intuitively, following the vector field from a noise sample $\mathbf{z}$ according to Equation~\ref{eq:ode} leads deterministically to exactly one target sample $\mathbf{x}$, and following it backwards in time from $\mathbf{x}$ leads back to exactly one noise sample, namely $\mathbf{z}$. VFM relies on this bijection: it pulls a conditional distribution over data back into noise space via the deterministic vector field. Because the pairing between noise and target samples is one to one, this procedure gives a well-defined distribution over noise. This would not be the case for diffusion models, whose sampling procedure is stochastic.

\subsubsection{Training Flow Maps with MeanFlow}
\label{subsubsec:meanflow}
Training a flow map is harder than training a flow model. A flow model describes the paths between noise and data only locally, through the velocity at each point, whereas a flow map must describe them globally, through the displacement between any two points on the same path \parencite{sander_MF}.

The predominant methods for training flow maps are derived from a simple rule that any valid flow map must satisfy, such as the Eulerian or Lagrangian consistency rule \parencite{sander_MF,boffi2025flowmapmatchingstochastic}. These rules relate the flow map to the underlying marginal vector field $v$, so the training objectives derived from them require access to $v$. Methods differ in how $v$ is provided. Distillation methods first train a flow model and use it to provide $v$ \parencite{song2023consistencymodels,boffi2025buildconsistencymodellearning}, whereas more recent methods avoid the need for a pretrained flow model and train the flow map from scratch.

MeanFlow \parencite{geng2025meanflowsonestepgenerative} is one such method. Its training objective, which we denote $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{MF}}$, is derived from the Eulerian consistency rule, and we give the derivation in Appendix~\ref{sec:meanflow_appendix}. The derivation relies on an alternative parameterisation of the flow map, through the average velocity over the interval from $s$ to $t$:
\begin{align}
V(\mathbf{x}_s,s,t)
&=
\frac{1}{t-s}\int_{s}^{t}v(\mathbf{x}_\tau,\tau)\,\mathrm{d}\tau
\label{eq:average_velocity}
\\
F(\mathbf{x}_s,s,t)
&=
\mathbf{x}_s+(t-s)\,V(\mathbf{x}_s,s,t)
\label{eq:flow_map_average_velocity}
\end{align}
Since each parameterisation can be obtained from the other, the two are equivalent. Since MeanFlow has shown to be state-of-the-art, we use flow maps trained with MeanFlow in all of our experiments: for the two-dimensional experiments, and a publicly released MeanFlow model for the image experiments.

\section{Variational Inference}
\label{sec:variational_inference}
In Section~\ref{sec:variational_flow_maps}, we will see that VFM formulates conditional generation with a flow map as inferring a posterior distribution over the noise space of the flow map. This posterior distribution can only be evaluated up to a normalising constant. Variational inference (VI) is the standard tool for approximating such distributions \parencite{Blei_2017}. This section introduces VI, first for a single observation and then in its amortised form, which both VFM and our method use. Our presentation roughly follows \textcite{pml2Book}.

\subsection{VI for a Single Observation}
\label{subsec:vi_single_observation}
Consider a probabilistic model with an unobserved latent variable $\mathbf{z}$, distributed according to a prior $p(\mathbf{z})$. In this model, an observation $\mathbf{y}$ is generated from a latent $\mathbf{z}\sim p(\mathbf{z})$ by sampling $\mathbf{y}\sim p(\mathbf{y}\mid\mathbf{z})$, where $p(\mathbf{y}\mid\mathbf{z})$ is called the likelihood. Suppose we are given an observation $\mathbf{y}$ and wish to determine which latent generated it. More precisely, in probabilistic modelling, we wish to determine the following posterior over latents which could have plausibly generated $\mathbf{y}$
\begin{equation}
p(\mathbf{z}\mid\mathbf{y})
=
\frac{p(\mathbf{y}\mid\mathbf{z})\,p(\mathbf{z})}{p(\mathbf{y})}
\qquad
p(\mathbf{y})
=
\int p(\mathbf{y}\mid\mathbf{z})\,p(\mathbf{z})\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{z}
\label{eq:vi_posterior}
\end{equation}

Computing $p(\mathbf{y})$ requires integrating over all latents, which is generally intractable. We therefore cannot evaluate $p(\mathbf{z}\mid\mathbf{y})$ exactly, nor sample from it. To overcome this, VI chooses a tractable family of distributions $q_{\psi}(\mathbf{z})$, parameterised by variational parameters $\psi$, and optimises $\psi$ so that $q_{\psi}(\mathbf{z})$ approximates $p(\mathbf{z}\mid\mathbf{y})$. Importantly, the variational family must allow both density evaluation and sampling. A common choice is the family of Gaussian distributions, in which case $\psi$ consists of a mean and a variance.

VI optimises $\psi$ by minimising the reverse KL divergence between $q_{\psi}(\mathbf{z})$ and $p(\mathbf{z}\mid\mathbf{y})$ with respect to $\psi$.
Using the definition of KL divergence (twice) and Bayes' rule,
\begin{align}
D_{\mathrm{KL}}\left(q_{\psi}(\mathbf{z})\parallel p(\mathbf{z}\mid\mathbf{y})\right)
&=
\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{z}\sim q_{\psi}}
\left[
\log q_{\psi}(\mathbf{z})
-\log p(\mathbf{y}\mid\mathbf{z})
-\log p(\mathbf{z})
\right]
+\log p(\mathbf{y})
\nonumber
\\
&=
-\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{z}\sim q_{\psi}}
\left[\log p(\mathbf{y}\mid\mathbf{z})\right]
+
D_{\mathrm{KL}}\left(q_{\psi}(\mathbf{z})\parallel p(\mathbf{z})\right)
+\log p(\mathbf{y})
\label{eq:vi_kl_decomposition}
\\
&=
-\mathrm{ELBO}(\psi;\mathbf{y})+\log p(\mathbf{y})
\nonumber
\end{align}

Here, $\mathrm{ELBO}(\psi;\mathbf{y}):=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{z}\sim q_{\psi}}\left[\log p(\mathbf{y}\mid\mathbf{z})\right]-D_{\mathrm{KL}}\left(q_{\psi}(\mathbf{z})\parallel p(\mathbf{z})\right)$ is the evidence lower bound. Since $\log p(\mathbf{y})$ does not depend on $\psi$, minimising the KL divergence is equivalent to maximising the ELBO, which requires only being able to evaluate the unnormalised posterior $p(\mathbf{y}\mid\mathbf{z})\,p(\mathbf{z})$.


This is the same reverse-KL objective used to train normalising flows in Equation~\ref{eq:normalising_flow_reverse_kl}, with $q_{\psi}$ in place of $p_{\phi}$ and the posterior as the target. The VI framework makes explicit that the normalising constant of the target is not required to minimise this objective. It also offers an interpretation specific to posterior inference. By Equation~\ref{eq:vi_kl_decomposition}, maximising the ELBO balances two goals: placing mass on latents that explain the observation well, by maximising the expected log-likelihood $\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{z}\sim q_{\psi}}\left[\log p(\mathbf{y}\mid\mathbf{z})\right]$, and keeping $q_{\psi}$ close to the prior, by minimising $D_{\mathrm{KL}}\left(q_{\psi}(\mathbf{z})\parallel p(\mathbf{z})\right)$.

\subsection{Amortised VI}
\label{subsec:amortised_vi}
In the probabilistic model defined above, each time we draw a latent $\mathbf{z}\sim p(\mathbf{z})$ and then sample an observation $\mathbf{y}\sim p(\mathbf{y}\mid\mathbf{z})$, the resulting observation varies, and each observation has its own posterior $p(\mathbf{z}\mid\mathbf{y})$.
The procedure of Section~\ref{subsec:vi_single_observation} fits a distribution $q_{\psi}(\mathbf{z})$ to one of these posteriors for a given $\mathbf{y}$, so the optimal parameters $\psi$ depend on the observation $\mathbf{y}$. Every time a new observation arrives, a new optimisation problem must therefore be solved to find the parameters of the approximating distribution. In an inverse problem, where every new measurement corresponds to a new observation, this makes posterior inference expensive at test time.

Amortised VI avoids this by learning a neural network $g_{\phi}$, with parameters $\phi$, that maps any observation $\mathbf{y}$ to the variational parameters $\psi=g_{\phi}(\mathbf{y})$ of the variational distribution $q_{\psi}(\mathbf{z})$ which approximates $p(\mathbf{z}\mid\mathbf{y})$. We write the resulting approximation as $q_{\phi}(\mathbf{z}\mid\mathbf{y}):=q_{\psi}(\mathbf{z})$, to make explicit that it depends on both the network parameters $\phi$ and the observation $\mathbf{y}$. Since the network must approximate the posterior of every observation the probabilistic model can produce, $\phi$ is trained to maximise the expected ELBO over observations,
\begin{align}
&\max_{\phi}\;
\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{y}\sim p(\mathbf{y})}
\left[\mathrm{ELBO}\left(g_{\phi}(\mathbf{y});\mathbf{y}\right)\right]
\nonumber
\\
&\qquad=
\max_{\phi}\;
\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{y}\sim p(\mathbf{y})}
\left[
\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{z}\sim q_{\phi}(\cdot\mid\mathbf{y})}
\left[\log p(\mathbf{y}\mid\mathbf{z})\right]
-
D_{\mathrm{KL}}\left(q_{\phi}(\mathbf{z}\mid\mathbf{y})\parallel p(\mathbf{z})\right)
\right]
\label{eq:amortised_objective}
\end{align}
where the second line follows by substituting $\psi=g_{\phi}(\mathbf{y})$ into the definition of the ELBO.
In practice, this expectation is estimated using a batch of observations (this is known as stochastic VI). In amortised VI, the cost of optimisation is thus paid once, during training, and shared across all observations. Hence approximating the posterior of a new observation requires only a single forward pass of the network rather than an entire new optimisation procedure. 

\section{Variational Flow Maps}
\label{sec:variational_flow_maps}
This section introduces Variational Flow Maps (VFM) \parencite{VFM}, the method that this thesis builds on. Recall that our goal is to solve inverse problems with a pretrained flow map, in a single network evaluation of the flow map. VFM addresses this by framing conditional generation as a probabilistic inference problem over the input noise space of the flow map, which it performs using amortised VI. We first explain this framing, then describe VFM's Gaussian noise adapter, and finally explain why VFM must fine-tune the pretrained flow map, which is the limitation that motivates our method.

\subsection{Conditional Generation in Noise Space}
\label{subsec:vfm_noise_space}
Recall the inverse problem setup from Section~\ref{subsec:inverse_problems}: an observation $\mathbf{y}=\mathcal{A}(\mathbf{x})+\boldsymbol{\eta}$ is produced from an unknown data sample $\mathbf{x}\sim p_{\mathrm{data}}$ by a forward operator $\mathcal{A}$ and measurement noise $\boldsymbol{\eta}$. Given $\mathbf{y}$, the goal is to generate samples from the posterior $p_{\mathrm{data}}(\mathbf{x}\mid\mathbf{y})$, i.e. plausible data samples that are consistent with $\mathbf{y}$.

Now suppose we have a pretrained unconditional flow map, whose one-step generator maps noise $\mathbf{z}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{I})$ to samples $\mathbf{x}=G_{\theta}(\mathbf{z})\sim p_{\theta}(\mathbf{x})$, where $p_{\theta}(\mathbf{x})\approx p_{\mathrm{data}}(\mathbf{x})$. Both VFM and this thesis aim to use this flow map to generate samples, also in a single step, from the posterior
\begin{equation}
p_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}\mid\mathbf{y})
\propto
p(\mathbf{y}\mid\mathbf{x})\,p_{\theta}(\mathbf{x})
\label{eq:model_posterior}
\end{equation}
which approximates $p_{\mathrm{data}}(\mathbf{x}\mid\mathbf{y})$. VFM achieves this by fine-tuning the flow map, whereas this thesis aims to do so while keeping the pretrained flow map frozen.

For flow models, this goal is typically achieved by guidance. Suppose we have a pretrained unconditional velocity network $v_{\theta}$, whose trajectories end at samples from $p_{\theta}(\mathbf{x})$. Guidance nudges the output of $v_{\theta}$ at every step of the sampling procedure, so that the trajectory instead ends at a sample from $p_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}\mid\mathbf{y})$ (Chapter~\ref{chap:related_work}).
A one-step generator has no such trajectory to nudge: once the noise $\mathbf{z}$ is chosen, $G_{\theta}$ transports it directly to the data sample $G_{\theta}(\mathbf{z})$.

The key insight of VFM \parencite{VFM} is that the starting noise is therefore the only remaining lever for controlling the generated sample. To generate samples from $p_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}\mid\mathbf{y})$, we can instead look for a distribution over the starting noise with the following property: if $\mathbf{z}$ is drawn from it, then $G_{\theta}(\mathbf{z})$ is distributed according to $p_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}\mid\mathbf{y})$. Because $G_{\theta}$ is a bijection between noise and data (Section~\ref{subsubsec:flow_map_bijection}), such a distribution exists and is unique: it is obtained by pulling $p_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}\mid\mathbf{y})$ back through $G_{\theta}$ into noise space. We denote this distribution as $p_{\theta}(\mathbf{z}\mid\mathbf{y})$. Drawing $\mathbf{z}$ from this noise distribution and applying $G_{\theta}$ yields samples from $p_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}\mid\mathbf{y})$.

Although we defined $p_{\theta}(\mathbf{z}\mid\mathbf{y})$ by pulling $p_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}\mid\mathbf{y})$ back through $G_{\theta}$, its density can be derived without the change-of-variables formula. Since the observation $\mathbf{y}$ depends on the noise $\mathbf{z}$ only through the generated sample $\mathbf{x}=G_{\theta}(\mathbf{z})$, and $G_{\theta}$ is deterministic, Bayes' rule in noise space gives
\begin{align}
p_{\theta}(\mathbf{z}\mid\mathbf{y})
&\propto
p(\mathbf{y}\mid G_{\theta}(\mathbf{z}))\,\mathcal{N}\left(\mathbf{z}\mid\mathbf{0},\mathbf{I}\right)
\nonumber
\\
&=
\mathcal{N}\left(\mathbf{y}\mid\mathcal{A}(G_{\theta}(\mathbf{z})),\sigma_y^2\mathbf{I}_{M}\right)
\,
\mathcal{N}\left(\mathbf{z}\mid\mathbf{0},\mathbf{I}\right)
\label{eq:noise_posterior}
\end{align}
where the second line substitutes the likelihood of Equation~\ref{eq:inverse_problem_likelihood}, evaluated at $G_{\theta}(\mathbf{z})$.

Our goal is to sample from $p_{\theta}(\mathbf{z}\mid\mathbf{y})$. However, we cannot do so directly because computing its normalising constant requires integrating over the entire noise space and so is intractable. Fortunately, because we can evaluate its density up to this constant according to Equation~\ref{eq:noise_posterior}, VI can be used to approximate it. Indeed, our setup is an instance of the probabilistic model we introduced in Section~\ref{subsec:vi_single_observation}, in which the noise $\mathbf{z}$ is the latent variable, with prior $\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{I})$ and likelihood $p(\mathbf{y}\mid G_{\theta}(\mathbf{z}))$.

\subsection{The Gaussian Noise Adapter}
\label{subsec:vfm_gaussian_adapter}
Since every observation $\mathbf{y}$ has its own noise posterior $p_{\theta}(\mathbf{z}\mid\mathbf{y})$, VFM approximates them all simultaneously with a single neural network, using amortised VI (Section~\ref{subsec:amortised_vi}). Recall that amortised VI trains a network $g_{\phi}$ which, given an observation $\mathbf{y}$, outputs the parameters of the variational distribution approximating its posterior. VFM calls this network the noise adapter, and chooses the variational family to be Gaussians with diagonal covariance. Given $\mathbf{y}$, the noise adapter therefore outputs a mean and a variance,
\begin{equation}
g_{\phi}(\mathbf{y})
=
\left(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\phi}(\mathbf{y}),\,\boldsymbol{\sigma}^2_{\phi}(\mathbf{y})\right)
\label{eq:noise_adapter}
\end{equation}
and the variational distribution approximating $p_{\theta}(\mathbf{z}\mid\mathbf{y})$ is
\begin{equation}
q_{\phi}(\mathbf{z}\mid\mathbf{y})
=
\mathcal{N}\left(\mathbf{z}\mid\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\phi}(\mathbf{y}),\,\mathrm{diag}\left(\boldsymbol{\sigma}^2_{\phi}(\mathbf{y})\right)\right)
\label{eq:gaussian_adapter}
\end{equation}
Diagonal Gaussians satisfy both requirements of VI. To draw a sample, we query the noise adapter for $\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\phi}(\mathbf{y})$ and $\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{\phi}(\mathbf{y})$, draw $\boldsymbol{\epsilon}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{I})$, and set $\mathbf{z}=\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\phi}(\mathbf{y})+\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{\phi}(\mathbf{y})\odot\boldsymbol{\epsilon}$. The density can also be evaluated exactly.

To learn $\phi$, VFM maximises the amortised objective of Equation~\ref{eq:amortised_objective}. This objective requires evaluating the prior and the log-likelihood of the probabilistic model. 
In the VFM setting, the prior is $p(\mathbf{z})=\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{I})$. Because $G_{\theta}$ is deterministic, knowing $\mathbf{z}$ is the same as knowing the generated sample $G_{\theta}(\mathbf{z})$. Conditioning on either one therefore gives the same distribution for $\mathbf{y}$, so the likelihood term of the VI model satisfies $p(\mathbf{y}\mid\mathbf{z})=p(\mathbf{y}\mid G_{\theta}(\mathbf{z}))$. We use the latter form to make the dependence on the flow map explicit. By Equation~\ref{eq:inverse_problem_likelihood}, its logarithm is
\begin{equation}
\log p(\mathbf{y}\mid G_{\theta}(\mathbf{z}))
=
-\frac{\lVert\mathbf{y}-\mathcal{A}(G_{\theta}(\mathbf{z}))\rVert_2^2}{2\sigma_y^2}
+\mathrm{const}
\label{eq:vfm_log_likelihood}
\end{equation}
where the constant does not depend on $\phi$. Substituting our prior and likelihood into Equation~\ref{eq:amortised_objective}, negating, and dropping the constant, we see that maximising the amortised ELBO objective is equivalent to minimising the loss
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{ELBO}}(\phi)
=
\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{y}\sim p(\mathbf{y})}
\left[
\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{z}\sim q_{\phi}(\cdot\mid\mathbf{y})}
\left[
\frac{\lVert\mathbf{y}-\mathcal{A}(G_{\theta}(\mathbf{z}))\rVert_2^2}{2\sigma_y^2}
\right]
+
D_{\mathrm{KL}}\left(q_{\phi}(\mathbf{z}\mid\mathbf{y})\parallel\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{I})\right)
\right]
\label{eq:vfm_elbo_loss}
\end{equation}
Following \textcite{VFM}, we refer to the first term as the observation loss and the second as the KL-to-prior loss. 
The observation loss rewards noise that the generator maps to samples consistent with $\mathbf{y}$, while the KL-to-prior loss keeps the noise adapter's output close to the standard normal inputs on which the flow map was trained. Since both $q_{\phi}(\mathbf{z}\mid\mathbf{y})$ and the prior are Gaussian, the KL-to-prior loss has a closed form and can be evaluated exactly.
In practice, the outer expectation over $\mathbf{y}$ is estimated using observations obtained by applying the forward problem to training data.

\subsection{Joint Training of the Noise Adapter and Flow Map}
\label{subsec:vfm_joint_training}
Although a diagonal Gaussian supports both sampling and density evaluation operations required by VI, it may be too simple to approximate the noise posterior $p_{\theta}(\mathbf{z}\mid\mathbf{y})$. The data posterior $p_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}\mid\mathbf{y})$ is often multimodal, and pulling a multimodal distribution back through a nonlinear flow map may give a noise posterior that is also multimodal, which a single Gaussian cannot represent. Moreover, the noise adapter is trained with the reverse-KL objective, which is mode-seeking (as discussed in Section~\ref{subsubsec:training_normalising_flows}), so it tends to collapse onto a single mode. \textcite{VFM} establish this failure both theoretically and empirically. 

VFM's solution is to fine-tune the flow map jointly with the noise adapter, so that the geometry of the noise space is reshaped such that $p_{\theta}(\mathbf{z}\mid\mathbf{y})$ can be better approximated by a simple Gaussian. 
This is possible because the ELBO objective depends on $\theta$ as well as $\phi$. Since the ELBO is a lower bound on the log-evidence $\log p_{\theta}(\mathbf{y})$, maximising it with respect to $\theta$ performs approximate maximum-likelihood estimation of the flow map. However, fine-tuning on the ELBO alone could turn $G_{\theta}$ into a map that is no longer a valid flow map. VFM therefore adds a term based on the MeanFlow loss $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{MF}}$ of Appendix~\ref{sec:meanflow_appendix}, giving the joint objective
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{VFM}}(\phi,\theta)
=
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{ELBO}}(\phi;\theta^{-})
+
\frac{1}{2\tau^2}\,\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{MF}}(\theta;\phi)
\label{eq:vfm_joint_objective}
\end{equation}
Here, $\tau$ weights the MeanFlow term. Two details of this objective matter in practice. First, for stability, the observation loss is evaluated using an exponential moving average $\theta^{-}$ of the flow map's parameters, rather than $\theta$ itself. The flow map's parameters are therefore updated only through the MeanFlow term. Second, the MeanFlow term is evaluated on noise drawn partly from the adapter, in a proportion $\alpha$, rather than only from the standard normal. This is what allows fine-tuning to reshape the noise space. We refer to \textcite{VFM} for a rigorous derivation of Equation~\ref{eq:vfm_joint_objective}, since we do not use this objective in our method.

To summarise, Joint training allows VFM to perform conditional generation in a single step with a simple Gaussian adapter, but at the cost of fine-tuning the flow map. 
Since the flow map is fine-tuned for a specific conditional generation setting, a new round of fine-tuning is required whenever the setting changes. For example when moving from inverse problems to conditioning on a text prompt.
This is expensive for large generators, and fine-tuning for one setting may degrade the flow map's ability to generate from the unconditional data distribution. \textcite{VFM} themselves suggest replacing the Gaussian adapter with a more expressive variational family, such as normalising flows, as future work. This thesis pursues this direction: we keep the pretrained flow map frozen, and instead make the noise adapter expressive enough to approximate the noise posterior on its own.
